Build-outcome predictors inside CI batching

Does a better build-outcome predictor actually save more CI runs once it decides which builds a batching workflow skips?

The question

Two ideas cut the cost of continuous integration. Build-outcome prediction guesses whether a build will pass so CI can skip it, and batch testing runs several changes in one execution and only bisects when a batch fails. Prediction-assisted forward batching (the ML-CI setup of Kamath et al.) combines them, so the predictor now decides which builds run at all.

If a build-outcome predictor scores better on its own, does it also save more CI runs once it controls that decision? And what happens to history-aware predictors when the workflow stops observing the builds it skips?

Study design

  • Data. 22 CircleCI projects from the RavenBuild dataset and 20 Java projects from TravisTorrent (February 2017 snapshot, last 400 eligible builds each). Each project is split chronologically: the first 75% for model fitting and validation (a further 75/25 split), the last 25% held out.
  • Predictors. BuildFast, an XGBoost model over 72 history-aware features. RavenBuild-CR, which adds 12 context and relevance features (84 in total) but drops the dependency-aware ones, since those cannot be rebuilt consistently across both datasets. Parrot, which simply repeats the previous build's outcome. Features were rebuilt with one shared pipeline, with AST features for TravisTorrent extracted using GumTree 3.0.0.
  • Simulation. Builds are processed in order. A predicted failure triggers batch formation, and a failing batch is diagnosed to find the culprit. Every feature is updated only from builds the workflow has actually executed, as it would be in a deployed CI system.
  • Configurations. Prediction thresholds of 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7. A timeout rule that forces an execution after 2, 4, 8 or 16 consecutive skips, or no timeout. Fixed batches of 4, 8 or 16, or Lightweight Dynamic batching (Exponential-2). BatchBisect, BatchStop4 or BatchDivide4 diagnosis.
  • Selection. For each predictor, project and batch formation, we picked the configuration with the highest validation savings whose initially missed failures stayed under 10%, 20% or 30%, then froze it and tested it on the held-out builds.

How we measured it

Execution savings is (N - E) / N × 100, where N is the number of eligible builds and E counts every simulated execution, including diagnosis reruns. Initially missed failures is the share of truly failing builds that were skipped. Never-detected failures is the share that no later executed batch on the same branch exposed.

Projects are the unit of analysis. We compared predictors with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and tested practical equivalence with paired Wilcoxon TOST at a ±2.5 percentage-point margin, with Holm correction and project-bootstrap confidence intervals. Effect sizes for predictor, batch formation and diagnosis come from partial η² in project-blocked models.

To explain the predictors, we grouped all 84 features into 15 families across 5 themes and labeled each by the observations it needs: independent (only the current change), structural (which earlier builds ran) or outcome-dependent (observed pass or fail results). We then summed TreeSHAP attributions per family for every prediction, called a family decisive when removing it flips the decision at a 0.5 threshold, and retrained both models without each family.

Finding 1: a better predictor did not save more

Under matched validation limits, BuildFast and RavenBuild-CR saved practically the same share of executions. TOST established equivalence within ±2.5 points on TravisTorrent at all three limits (Holm p ≤ 0.022), and within ±5 points on CircleCI (Holm p ≤ 0.004), where BuildFast saved slightly more at every limit.

The feature analysis explains why. Failure history alone carried 33 to 40% of each model's TreeSHAP attribution, and the outcome-dependent families together carried 58 to 63%. RavenBuild-CR's 12 extra features took only 7 to 9% of its decision weight, and retraining it without them lowered AUC by just 0.01 (BH p ≥ 0.75). Parrot, the one-line baseline, saved 5.3 to 6.7 points more than the learned models on CircleCI but missed 8 to 12 points more failures.

Finding 2: batching starves the predictors

A history-aware predictor learns from builds it has seen fail. Once batching skips a build, its outcome is never observed, so features such as the previous outcome, failure ratios and builds since the last pass freeze at their last value. A stale "all green" view keeps predicting success. We call this observation starvation.

  • Without a timeout, at a 0.5 threshold, 33% (BuildFast) and 39% (RavenBuild-CR) of configurations on CircleCI, and 70% and 72% on TravisTorrent, never executed a single batch.
  • Failure history decided 14% of BuildFast's predictions when every outcome was observed, but only 4% on CircleCI and 1% on TravisTorrent under batching.
  • The starting state largely decided the outcome: 49% of CircleCI and 75% of TravisTorrent configurations never triggered when the last observed build had passed, against 7% and 0% when it had failed.
  • Without batching, removing the outcome-dependent families barely moved AUC but cut failure recall by 25 points on CircleCI (BH p = 0.007) and by 13 to 15 points on TravisTorrent. The information that lets the predictors catch failures is exactly what batching hides.

Finding 3: workflow structure beats model choice

Batch formation explained far more of the variation in savings (partial η² = 0.265 on CircleCI and 0.112 on TravisTorrent) than the choice of predictor (0.002 and under 0.001). Failure diagnosis also mattered more (0.098 and 0.044), and BatchDivide4 saved significantly more than BatchBisect and BatchStop4 on both datasets.

Finding 4: validation limits did not hold on unseen builds

Configurations chosen to miss at most 10% of failures on validation builds missed 16.7% (BuildFast) and 14.2% (RavenBuild-CR) on held-out CircleCI builds, and 33.1% and 36.1% on TravisTorrent. At that limit, 32 of 64 BuildFast and 28 of 67 RavenBuild-CR configurations on CircleCI broke it, as did 27 of 47 and 28 of 48 on TravisTorrent.

Five TravisTorrent projects had no failing validation builds at all. In three of them, the selected configuration later skipped 93 to 100% of the failures in the test period.

Can better design fix it?

We tested three redesigns at thresholds of 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7: an observation-robust model trained without outcome-dependent features, a family-aware trigger that ignores stale outcome evidence, and an accumulated-risk trigger that fires once the combined failure probability of skipped changes crosses the threshold.

All three reduced starvation. The observation-robust BuildFast cut never-triggering configurations from 33% to 18% on CircleCI and from 70% to 42% on TravisTorrent. None beat the trade-off that thresholds and timeouts already reach, though: at 0.5 they stayed 0.3 to 2.6 points below that frontier, and Parrot with a timeout sat above every learned configuration on both datasets.

In ongoing work on batching without skipping, every build is eventually tested and the cost becomes detection delay. Predictor-free batching already saves 17.9% (CircleCI) and 50.6% (TravisTorrent) of executions at a mean delay of at most two builds. The learned predictors add at most 4.5 and 2.0 points, and a simple per-project failure-rate rule matches most of that gain.

Takeaway

When prediction is combined with batching, observation is the main lever, not model choice. CI teams should set a timeout and tune batch formation and diagnosis before paying for a more accurate predictor. Evaluations of history-aware predictors should report never-detected failures and how often a configuration never triggers, not just standalone accuracy.

The manuscript is in preparation, with a replication package covering the feature pipeline, the batching simulator and the analysis scripts.
